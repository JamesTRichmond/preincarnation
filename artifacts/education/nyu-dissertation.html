The Second Sentence:
Reincarnation as Save System in PREINCARNATION

Grok Build

11 September 2026

Abstract

Games that wear The Legend of Zelda: A Link to the Past as a coat, and nothing else, fail a reviewer’s first test: they have no second sentence. PREINCARNATION began as that coat — Vermont towns, SNES cells, a sword on Space — and then accrued systems (skins, GPS, an iPod, a spherical afterlife) until the person paying for the work could no longer say what to fix. This dissertation argues that the correction is not more content. It is a playable claim: the player is one person; death is a chapter; the Marble Vein is the only plot. Drawing on Utah’s craft curriculum (level design, juice, ethics), USC’s playcentric production (character, world, vertical slice), and the NYU Game Center’s insistence that games are a cultural form and that design itself is research (Zimmerman; Lantz; Juul), I treat PREINCARNATION as the dissertation: a diegetic loop in which reincarnation is the save system, the character sheet is the avatar, and God or the Devil is the loading screen. The written component records the diagnosis, the literature, the method, and the before/after of the work that shipped.

1. The problem of the coat

The original proposal for PREINCARNATION is ambitious and split. Death is “the ultimate win condition.” The soul becomes a universe. Vermont is the mortal floor; the Soloverse is the ceiling. That is a second sentence. What shipped, for a long stretch, was the first sentence only: a top-down 16-bit adventure with hearts in the corner labeled LIFE, a wanderer who still read as Link, and a Limbo that offered other bodies — smuggler, officer, embryo — as if reincarnation were a character-select screen.

Feature accretion hid the split. A strange device that plays the player’s own music. Weather from a geolocated county. Twenty towns. Bottles. A spectrum of fact and fiction. Each was defensible. Together they made a kitchen. The person commissioning the game said they had lost the thread and did not know what order to give next. That sentence is a user-research finding. When the producer cannot name the verb, the game has plateaued.

Reviews of sibling games already named the failure modes (compiled as studio notes before this pass): Blossom Tales and Oceanhorn are punished for wallpaper; Hades is praised because every death advances a person, a rumor, or a room; Tunic is loved and hated for opacity; Death’s Door is the counter-example, every detour looping back to a door you already own. PREINCARNATION was collecting the wallpaper and postponing the Hades lesson.

Clone with no second sentence. Death that erases the afternoon. Combat that is only stand-and-slash. A map that is wide and empty.

Those four lines are the plateaus. The degrees are how you leave them.

2. Literature and faculty traditions

2.1 Utah — craft is not optional

The University of Utah’s B.S. in Games (GAMES 2100, 3100, 3710/3720, 4500/4510) treats a game as a made object with a pipeline, a level, and a capstone that must actually play. Steve Swink’s Game Feel and Vlambeer’s juice talks sit here even when they are not on the syllabus: every verb needs disproportionate, multi-sensory feedback, and the feedback must not change the sim. Trauma² screenshake (Eiserloh), hitstop of 30–120ms, camera lookahead, pitch-randomized impact. Utah also requires ethics (GAMES 3020) and storycrafting (GAMES 3035): a death loop that erases the player’s afternoon is not clever; it is rude.

2.2 USC — playcentric, then cut

USC Games (CTIN 541, 532, CTWR 410) trains a producer’s cruelty. Prototype. Playtest. Cut what does not serve the fantasy. Fullerton’s playcentric design is the method: the player’s feeling is the spec. CTWR 410’s useful bluntness: if the character is a skin, there is no character. Interactive Experience and World Design (CTIN 532) asks whether the space teaches the next verb. A Green with Maeve unmarked and a Vein unnamed is not world design. It is a tilemap.

2.3 NYU — games as cultural form

Frank Lantz’s Game Center frames games as a form, not a product category. Eric Zimmerman’s professional practice and thesis courses insist that design is research: you learn the argument by making the thing that argues. Jesper Juul’s The Art of Failure is the reincarnation paper hiding in the library — failure that the player consents to, that the system remembers. Hades operationalizes it. A dissertation that is only a document about a game is a missed chance; the game must be the argument, the document the apparatus.

2.4 Adjacent work

Hunicke, LeBlanc, and Zubek’s MDA (mechanics–dynamics–aesthetics) is used here in reverse: start from the aesthetic (one life, sent back, a job that outlives the body), derive the dynamics (cycle memory, God/Devil as a function of karma and flaw), then the mechanics (sheet, graves, last deed). Salen and Zimmerman’s Rules of Play on meaningful choice: a bond that never speaks is not a bond. Anthropy’s Rise of the Videogame Zinesters on voice: Vermont dry, not a father-quest. Miyamoto’s dungeon craft, via ALttP and ALBW: a tool is not a key with extra pixels.

3. Method

The method is a vertical slice with a thesis, not a content roadmap.

  1. Name the fantasy in one breath. You are a Vermonter. You will die. God, or the other one, will send you back as yourself to wake the Marble Vein.
  2. Cut the costume shop. Reincarnation is not a new sprite. It is fourteen winters and the same hands.
  3. Give the sheet teeth. Background, bond, ideal, and flaw must change a line, a threshold, or a number, or they are menu fiction.
  4. Make death a chapter. NPCs remember the year and the name. The last deed is spoken in the afterlife. A grave holds the last skin.
  5. Juice the only verb that is not walking. The sword must feel like iron. Hitstop, flash, knockback, layered SFX, floating numbers, camera kick.
  6. Mark the next verb. Maeve glows until you talk. The shovel glows until you take it. The plaque names the job. The bottom line names the step.

Playtests in this studio are agent-driven: a first life is created, the intro is walked, the Green is entered, death is forced toward both hosts, return is checked for the same body. The producer is not asked to be QA.

4. The playable argument

PREINCARNATION’s claim, after the education pass:

Reincarnation is the save system. Dying writes a grave, advances the calendar fourteen to eighteen years, awards skill, and opens a conversation with a host selected by karma (and by the flaw “the other voice,” which lowers the threshold for the Devil). Loading is “Finish the job.” The body on the other side is the one you made on the first screen.

The character sheet is the avatar. The SNES HUD no longer says LIFE. It says the name. Maeve says the name. Cal says the name. Bond changes the first conversation (Maeve’s lantern, Cal’s table). Ideal changes the economy of violence (Mercy refuses karma from kills; Finish it awards an extra point if you die with the Vein still quiet). Flaw changes gait and afterlife (Cannot rest walks faster; the blade hits harder; the other voice is already on a first-name basis with the Devil).

The county is a memory, not a wipe. After a death, Maeve does not give the tutorial again. She says the year and that she watched the body go cold. That is the Hades lesson in a Vermont mouth.

The Vein is the only plot. Side systems (the strange device, the spectrum, the bottles) may remain as county texture. They are not the sentence. The plaque at the top of the world is the sentence.

What was not cut: the strange device (the player already loved it), the Soloverse (the proposal’s ceiling), the county’s width (too late to unmake, and graves need ground). What was refused: another town, another skin, another system that does not speak the name.

6. Before and after, keeping the soul

The soul is the proposal’s mortal floor: Addison County, death as a door, karma as weather, a job that outlives a body. The after-education game still begins on the Green, still uses SNES cells, still lets a house stay unlocked with a rifle on the wall. It still has no credits sequence that owns you.

It no longer pretends you are Link. It no longer offers you a different face when you die. It no longer treats Maeve as a kiosk that forgets. The sword finally has weight. The afterlife finally has something specific to say about the life you just left.

If the producer is still lost, the next order is not “add.” It is “play the first death, then the second, and listen to Maeve.” If she does not sound like she knows you, the dissertation failed. If she does, the thread is back.
